I've watched people spend five figures on certifications that didn't change their salary by a dollar. The cybersecurity certification market runs on a simple formula: manufacture anxiety about falling behind, sell the cure, repeat next year when the exam version increments.

Some certs are worth it. Most aren't. The difference isn't the curriculum — it's whether hiring managers actually filter on that credential.

## The filter that matters

Here's what certification marketing won't tell you: a cert's value has almost nothing to do with what you learn and almost everything to do with whether it appears in job postings as a requirement.

CompTIA Security+ is not the most impressive security credential. The exam is multiple-choice. The content is broad but shallow. And it shows up in roughly 70% of entry-level cybersecurity job postings in the US. It's also DoD 8140 approved, which means government contractors can't hire you without it.

That's the whole case for Security+. Not the curriculum. The filter.

At $425 and two to three months of prep, it's the cheapest way to get past the HR screen that stops most applicants. If you're starting from zero, you get this one first. Not because it's the best — because the system demands it and fighting the system costs more than the cert.

## The one that pays for itself in two months

CISSP is the credential senior security people carry. Median total compensation for US holders is around $164,000. The exam costs $749. You need five years of paid experience across at least two of the eight domains.

The math is straightforward: if CISSP gets you a $15,000 raise, it pays for itself in two months. Few investments in this industry have that kind of return.

But there's a timing problem. Take it too early and you fail — not because the material is hard, but because the exam assumes you've already made the mistakes it's testing you on. It's a management exam dressed as a technical one. The questions aren't about what the right answer is. They're about what the right answer is *given that you're the person accountable for the outcome*.

Also: as of April 2026, ISC2 cut the experience waiver list from roughly 50 certifications to 25. CEH, CISA, CRISC, and OSCP got dropped. If you were counting on one of those to shave a year off the five-year requirement, you need a new plan.

## The one where you can't fake it

OSCP+ costs $1,749 and includes the PEN-200 course. The exam is 24 hours of breaking into machines followed by a written report. There's no multiple choice. There's no partial credit for showing your work. You either compromise the targets or you don't.

The fail rate is high and OffSec doesn't publish it, which tells you what you need to know.

Penetration testing hiring managers treat OSCP+ as a shortcut. A candidate without it might be great, but proving that takes an hour of technical questions. A candidate with it has already demonstrated the thing you're hiring for. That's the value — not the letters, the signal.

## Management certs: useful but mistimed

CISM is what you get when you're moving from doing security to running security programs. Governance, risk management, incident response strategy. The exam costs $575 to $760 and you need five years of security management experience.

The mistake people make with CISM is getting it too early. A CISM without management experience reads like someone who bought the answer key. A CISM after three years of running a team reads like someone who formalized what they already knew.

Pair it with CISSP and you've covered the two things CISO hiring committees actually care about: technical depth and management capability. But the order matters. CISSP first, manage for a few years, then CISM. Reverse it and you're the person with management certs and no management experience, which is a harder sell than no certs at all.

## The free one

ISC2 Certified in Cybersecurity (CC) is currently free under the 1MCC program. It's entry-level. It won't get you a senior role. But it costs nothing and comes from the same organization that issues CISSP.

For students, career changers, or anyone who wants to test whether cybersecurity is actually interesting before dropping $400 on Security+, this is the right starting point. The worst case is you waste a few weeks. With Security+, the worst case is you waste $425 and a few weeks.

## Career paths that don't waste your time

Most people collect certs the wrong way — they chase the impressive ones first and fill gaps later. The people who move fastest do the opposite: they get the cert that unlocks the next role, then stop.

SOC analyst: Security+ → CySA+ → SC-200 or Splunk. You're building from general security knowledge to detection and response to tool-specific expertise. Each step maps to a job title change.

Penetration testing: Security+ → PenTest+ or CEH → OSCP+. The first two teach vocabulary. OSCP+ proves you can operate. Skip the middle step if you're already comfortable with the terminology and go straight to the one that matters.

Cloud security: AWS Solutions Architect Associate → AWS Security Specialty. Cloud security is its own discipline. Generic security certs don't cover IAM misconfigurations or S3 bucket policies, and those are what actually get breached.

Management: Security+ → CISSP → CISM. Technical foundation, then the credential that signals seniority, then the one that signals leadership. Three certs across a decade-plus career. That's not a lot.

GRC: Security+ → CISA → CISM → CIPP/US. Governance, risk, and compliance is a distinct track that pays well and burns people out less often than the SOC. The CIPP/US is the odd one here — it's a privacy law cert, not a security cert — but GRC roles increasingly expect you to speak both languages.

## The certs that aren't worth your own money

GIAC certs are excellent. GSEC and GPEN in particular cover their domains thoroughly. They also cost up to $9,949 when you include the required SANS course. Employers pay for these. If you're paying out of pocket, there's almost always a cheaper cert that opens the same doors. Don't spend ten grand of your own money on something your employer should be funding.

EC-Council CEH has brand recognition but a weak reputation among practitioners. The exam is multiple-choice and doesn't test hands-on skills. It'll get past some HR filters. It won't impress a technical interviewer. At $950 to $1,199, you're paying for name recognition that's been declining for years.

## One cert per stage

The people who list eight certifications in their email signature are usually compensating for something. The people who get promoted have one or two that match exactly what their role demands next.

Entry-level: Security+ or CC. Mid-career: CySA+, PenTest+, or a cloud security specialty. Senior: CISSP or CISM.

Pick the next one, pass it, and move on. The cert industry wants you collecting credentials forever. Your career needs you to stop after the one that unlocks the next door.
